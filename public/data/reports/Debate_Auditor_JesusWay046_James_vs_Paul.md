**Steelman (each side):**

*Pro-speakers' position:* The strongest version of this argument is that genuine theological tensions existed between Paul and the Jerusalem church (which mainstream scholarship confirms), that James 2:14-26 likely engages and corrects a (mis)application of Pauline faith-language (which many scholars accept), and that Paul's letters reveal real conflicts with Jewish Christian authority figures that the book of Acts attempts to smooth over (which is widely acknowledged in critical scholarship). The parallels between specific James passages and Pauline formulations are real and noteworthy, and the argument from silence regarding Paul's collection is a legitimate (if not conclusive) scholarly observation.

*Pro-Paul position:* Paul operated within a complex first-century context of Jewish and Gentile integration, adapting his message for different audiences as a missionary strategy, not deception. His theology of grace and faith was meant to complement, not abolish, ethical living (Galatians 5:6: "faith working through love"). The apparent contradictions between Paul and James reflect different audiences and rhetorical situations rather than fundamental theological opposition, and the early church ultimately accepted both as canonical precisely because they were seen as complementary. Paul's own statements affirming the law (Romans 3:31, 7:12, 13:8-10) undermine the one-dimensional reading of Paul as anti-law.

### 2. The Ascents of James is a "pre-70 text"

### 3. Romans 3:7 shows Paul admitting to and justifying lying
**Rating:** Misleading — High confidence
Romans 3:7 is universally recognized by Pauline scholars as a passage of rhetorical diatribe — a common Greco-Roman rhetorical device where the author voices a hypothetical objector's argument to refute it. Paul is not speaking in his own voice endorsing lying. The very next verse (3:8) shows Paul rejecting this reasoning: "Their condemnation is deserved."
**Evidence:** Stowers, *A Rereading of Romans* (1994); Jewett, *Romans* (Hermeneia, 2007).
**What would settle it:** Reading any critical commentary on Romans 3:1-8.

### 5. Pliny the Younger killed Christians specifically for refusing to eat meat sacrificed to idols
**Rating:** Misleading — High confidence
Pliny's letter to Trajan (Epistulae X.96, c. 110 CE) describes testing suspected Christians by having them invoke Roman gods, offer incense and wine to Trajan's image, and curse Christ. He does not describe forcing Christians to eat meat sacrificed to idols as the specific test. The letter mentions temples had been "almost deserted" and sacrificial meat found "very few purchasers," but the execution was for refusal of Roman religious worship, not specifically for refusing to eat sacrificial meat.
**Evidence:** Pliny, *Epistulae* X.96 (full text available in multiple translations).
**What would settle it:** Reading Pliny's actual letter to Trajan.
**Timestamp + quote:** 2:38:50 — **"He was forcing them to eat flesh sacrificed to idols. And he was killing Christians if they would not eat the sacrificial meat."**

### 6. Acts is "unanimously" recognized in scholarship as "an apology for Paul"
**Rating:** Misleading — Medium confidence
While many scholars recognize a pro-Pauline tendency in Acts, the claim of "unanimous" consensus is overstated. Scholars disagree about Acts' primary purpose: theological historiography (Conzelmann), political apologetic for Christianity to Rome (Walaskay), narrative theology of the Spirit's work (Tannehill), and legitimation of Gentile Christianity (Esler). Paul-apologetic is one strand among many.
**Evidence:** Marshall, "Acts and the Former Treatise" in *The Book of Acts in Its Ancient Literary Setting* (1993).
**What would settle it:** A survey of Acts scholarship showing the range of proposed purposes.
**Timestamp + quote:** 19:46 — **"It's clearly an apology for Paul. There's no question about that unanimous position in scholarship."**

### 7. 1 Corinthians 9:19-23 is an admission of deliberate deception
**Rating:** Misleading — High confidence
Pauline scholars recognize this passage as describing missionary accommodation — adapting one's approach to different audiences — a common strategy in both Jewish and Greco-Roman rhetorical traditions. The passage does not describe deception about beliefs but flexibility in cultural practice. This interpretive framework is the overwhelming scholarly consensus.
**Evidence:** Thiselton, *The First Epistle to the Corinthians* (NIGTC, 2000); Fee, *The First Epistle to the Corinthians* (NICNT, 1987).
**What would settle it:** Reading scholarly commentaries on 1 Corinthians 9.

### 8. E.P. Sanders concluded Paul is "completely unintelligible"
**Rating:** Likely false — High confidence
E.P. Sanders' landmark *Paul and Palestinian Judaism* (1977) argued that Paul's theology was coherent within its own framework of "participationist eschatology." Sanders did note tensions in Paul's thought but concluded Paul was intelligible within his context. The "unintelligible" claim likely confuses Sanders with Albert Schweitzer's earlier observation about the difficulty of Paul's mysticism, but even Schweitzer did not call Paul "completely unintelligible."
**Evidence:** Sanders, *Paul and Palestinian Judaism* (1977); Schweitzer, *The Mysticism of Paul the Apostle* (1931).
**What would settle it:** Reading Sanders' conclusions in *Paul and Palestinian Judaism*.

### 9. Paul admitted to robbery: "I robbed other churches"
**Rating:** Misleading — High confidence
2 Corinthians 11:8 uses the verb esylēsa ("I robbed/plundered") in a clearly hyperbolic, ironic context — part of the "fool's speech" (2 Cor 11-12) where Paul employs ironic self-parody. The word means he accepted financial support from other congregations to minister to the Corinthians without charging them. Reading this as a literal confession of theft ignores the entire rhetorical context of the passage.
**Evidence:** Furnish, *II Corinthians* (Anchor Bible, 1984); Thrall, *2 Corinthians* (ICC, 2000).
**What would settle it:** Reading the passage in context with any major commentary.
**Timestamp + quote:** 2:41:07 — **"Paul admitted to robbery... 'I robbed other churches by taking wages from them to serve you.'"**

### 10. The Nazarenes rejected all liturgical commandments except the Ten Commandments
**Rating:** Misleading — Medium confidence
Patristic sources (Epiphanius, *Panarion* 29; Jerome, various letters) describe the Nazarenes as Torah-observant Jewish Christians who kept the entire Mosaic law including circumcision, Sabbath, and dietary laws — not just the Ten Commandments. This claim conflates Nazarene practice with a much later Protestant distinction between moral and ceremonial law.
**Evidence:** Epiphanius, *Panarion* 29.7; Jerome, *Epistle* 112; Skarsaune & Hvalvik, *Jewish Believers in Jesus* (2007).
