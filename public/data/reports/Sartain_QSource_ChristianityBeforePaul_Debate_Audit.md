## Section B — Rhetorical Stratagems & Weasel-Word Flags

### Stratagem 1 — Extension of Premises Beyond Natural Limits (HIGH SEVERITY)

**Page:** pp.1–2, throughout
**Quote:** *"The aim of this paper is to convincingly demonstrate that Q represents the earliest and most authentic strand of the Jesus movement prior to the Pauline strand that came to dominate modern Christianity."*

Q's existence as a written document is the dominant but contested working hypothesis in synoptic scholarship. The paper extends this hypothesis into a foundation for all subsequent claims — Q predating Mark, Q preserving "authentic" teaching, Q representing the "original" religion — without acknowledging that each step compounds the uncertainty of the previous one. By the time the paper reaches its conclusions about Paul inventing Christianity, the argument stands several inferential layers removed from established evidence.

### Stratagem 6 — Petitio Principii / Circular Reasoning (HIGH SEVERITY)

**Page:** pp.7, 13
**Quote:** *"one can only conclude that it must have been written prior to Mark, which was heavily influenced by Pauline soteriology and eschatology."*

The argument is structurally circular: Q predates Mark because it lacks Pauline elements; Pauline elements in Mark are identified by comparison to Paul's letters; but whether Q predates Paul is precisely what the argument is attempting to prove. Kloppenborg himself treats Q's stratification as a working hypothesis. The paper presents the conclusion as logical necessity without resolving the circularity.

### Stratagem 30 — Appeal to Authority Rather Than Reason (MEDIUM SEVERITY)

**Page:** pp.5–7, 12–13, 16–18
**Quote:** *"John Kloppenborg argues convincingly that Q was in fact a written source employed by both Matthew and Luke…the only logical conclusion based upon all the available evidence is that Q existed in a written form."*

Kloppenborg is repeatedly positioned as settling debates rather than advancing a position within an active scholarly conversation. ### Stratagem 3 — Extending Meaning Beyond Intended Sense (MEDIUM SEVERITY)

**Page:** p.7
**Quote:** *"Paul states that he received the blood and body symbolism in the Eucharist by means of a revelation directly from Jesus…he simply invented it out of whole cloth based upon a purported private vision."*

1 Corinthians 11:23's phrase "received from the Lord" uses *paralambanō/paradidōmi* — the same transmission vocabulary Paul employs in 1 Corinthians 15:3 for received communal tradition ("what I in turn had received"). This formula is standard in both Hellenistic and Jewish pedagogy for inherited tradition. The paper reads "from the Lord" as a confession of private invention, a meaning the phrase does not require and that the context does not support.

---

### Stratagem 35 — Appeal to Attributed Motive (MEDIUM SEVERITY)

**Page:** p.13
**Quote:** *"Paul intentionally chose not to emphasize Jesus's wisdom teachings because he knew those teachings completely contradicted his own visionary (some would say hallucinatory) post resurrection Christ."*

No direct evidence supports intentionality. The observable pattern — Paul rarely quotes Jesus's sayings — is extended to a psychological claim about deliberate suppression. Paul's letters focus on the crucified and risen Christ, but this may reflect pastoral emphasis, community context, or different catechetical traditions rather than conscious suppression. The parenthetical "(some would say hallucinatory)" is editorializing that belongs in a blog post, not academic argumentation.

## Section F — Detailed Claim Review

**1. Mark copied the Eucharist from 1 Corinthians 11:23 (Claim 11)**
**Rating:** FALSE | Confidence: 82%
**Evidence:** The verbal parallel between 1 Cor 11:23–25 and Mark 14:22–24 is acknowledged by mainstream NT scholars. However, direction of dependence is actively debated. Both texts may derive from independent liturgical tradition in early Christian communities. Scholars including Bauckham, Dunn, and Jeremias argue for pre-Pauline eucharistic tradition; Paul's own *paralambanō* formula (1 Cor 11:23; 15:3) signals received, not invented, tradition.
**What would settle it:** A demonstrated terminus ante quem for the pre-Markan Passion narrative that predates 1 Corinthians; or a clear literary dependency analysis ruling out independent liturgical tradition.
**Page 7 Quote:** *"Mark copies—nearly word for word—the earliest written account of the Eucharist as it is found in 1 Corinthians (ca. 50 CE)."*

**2. No historical or textual evidence for Nazareth during Jesus's lifetime (Claim 16)**
**Rating:** FALSE | Confidence: 92%
**Evidence:** Directly contradicted by archaeology. Zvi Gal's 1992 survey identified Roman-period remains at the site. Ken Dark's Nazareth Archaeological Project (2006–2020) documented first-century CE domestic structures beneath the Sisters of Nazareth Convent, consistent with a small village. "No historical or textual evidence" is a factual overclaim that does not engage current excavation reports.
**What would settle it:** The claim is effectively refuted by published excavation data. Engagement with Dark (2021) would be required to revive it.
**Page 8 Quote:** *"since there is no historical or textual evidence that a town by the name Nazaret or Nazareth existed during Jesus's lifetime, it is far more likely that the name refers to someone from the region known as the Tetrarchy of the Nazerini."*

**3. Q existed as a written document predating Mark (Claims 9, 23)**
**Rating:** DISPUTED | Confidence: 70%
**Evidence:** Q-as-written is the dominant Two-Source Hypothesis position. However, the Farrer-Goodacre hypothesis (Luke knew Matthew; Q never existed as a document) has significant scholarly support. More critically, most Q scholars date Q to 50–70 CE — overlapping with Paul's letters rather than clearly predating Pauline influence. The claim that Q predates Mark specifically requires argument the paper does not fully provide.
**What would settle it:** Discovery of an actual Q manuscript; or more conclusive literary dependency analysis showing Mark used Q rather than vice versa.
**Page 7 Quote:** *"It is clear that Q existed as a written source prior to its usage in Luke and Matthew."*

**4. Paul invented blood atonement doctrine (Claim 1)**
**Rating:** DISPUTED | Confidence: 72%
**Evidence:** Paul's letters undeniably emphasize atonement theology. However, 1 Corinthians 15:3 — "I handed on to you what I in turn had received: that Christ died for our sins" — uses received-tradition language, suggesting Paul inherited rather than originated this theology. Isaiah 53's Suffering Servant provides prior scriptural grounding. Early pre-Pauline formulas (Rom 3:25; 1 Cor 15:3–5) show signs of being received tradition, not Pauline composition.
**What would settle it:** Demonstration that no pre-Pauline community held atonement theology and that Paul's formula in 1 Cor 15:3 originated with him rather than being received tradition.
**Page 1 Quote:** *"this doctrine did not come from Jesus himself nor any of his direct disciples; instead, it was invented by the apostle Paul several years after Jesus's crucifixion."*

**5. The Didache dates to approximately 70 CE and is pre-Pauline (Claim 13)**
**Rating:** DISPUTED | Confidence: 76%
**Evidence:** Didache dating is genuinely contested. The mainstream scholarly range is 80–120 CE (Niederwimmer, Draper, van de Sandt). Milavec argues for 50–70 CE, but this is a minority position. The Didache may preserve traditions that predate Paul while being composed later. Calling it "pre-Pauline" conflates the age of individual traditions with the date of the document's composition.
**What would settle it:** Linguistic, textual, or archaeological evidence that permits more definitive dating; the Didache's relationship to Matthew in particular is a key contested datum.
**Page 7–8:** *"another pre-Pauline text written in approximately 70 CE known as the Didache, or The Teaching of the Twelve Apostles."*

**6. Paul intentionally suppressed Jesus's wisdom teachings (Claim 25)**
**Rating:** FALSE | Confidence: 87%
**Evidence:** No evidence supports intentionality. Paul's letters focus on the crucified Christ, but this reflects theological emphasis, pastoral context, and audience — not demonstrated suppression. Paul's communities in Corinth are the very context where wisdom teachers existed and were not suppressed; Paul argued with them theologically (1 Cor 1–4). The parenthetical "(some would say hallucinatory)" introduces ridicule without citation.
**What would settle it:** A Pauline text explicitly referencing and rejecting Jesus's wisdom sayings; absent that, intentionality is an inference projected onto a pattern.
**Page 13 Quote:** *"Paul intentionally chose not to emphasize Jesus's wisdom teachings because he knew those teachings completely contradicted his own visionary (some would say hallucinatory) post resurrection Christ."*

## Section H — Corroborated Claims

1. **Matthew and Luke used Mark as a primary source** — Markan priority represents near-universal scholarly consensus in NT source criticism. *Confirmed by:* Streeter, *The Four Gospels* (1924); Kloppenborg, *Q the Earliest Gospel* (2008); International Q Project.

2. **The canonical gospels were written at least 40 years after Jesus's crucifixion** — Mark is dated to approximately 70 CE, Matthew and Luke to approximately 80–90 CE, by mainstream scholarship. *Confirmed by:* Brown, *An Introduction to the New Testament* (1997).

3. **Q material is arranged differently in Matthew vs. Luke** — Luke's Q material clusters in two blocks (6:20–7:35; 9:57–13:34); Matthew distributes it across five major discourses. This distribution pattern is textbook Q scholarship and supports the argument against Luke knowing Matthew. *Confirmed by:* Kloppenborg (2008), 6–7; Wenham (1977), 141.

4. **Q does not contain passion or resurrection narratives** — By definition, the Q reconstruction (drawn from double tradition absent in Mark) contains no narrative of Jesus's death or post-resurrection appearances. *Confirmed by:* International Q Project reconstructions; Kloppenborg (2008).

5. **Matthew 7:21 and 7:24–27 are attributed to Q** — These sayings appear in Matthew and Luke (Luke 6:46; 6:47–49) and are attributed to Q by the overwhelming majority of synoptic scholars. *Confirmed by:* IQP critical edition; Robinson, Hoffmann, and Kloppenborg, *The Critical Edition of Q* (2000).

6. **Paul's letters teach faith-based justification over works of the law** — Romans 3:20, 5:1; Galatians 2:16 are unambiguous on justification by faith. *Confirmed by:* Dunn, *The Theology of Paul the Apostle* (1998); Sanders, *Paul and Palestinian Judaism* (1977).

7. **Matthew 2:23 fulfills no identifiable Old Testament prophecy** — No OT passage directly states "He shall be called a Nazorean." Widely acknowledged in major commentaries. *Confirmed by:* France, *The Gospel of Matthew* (NICNT, 2007); Keener, *A Commentary on the Gospel of Matthew* (1999).

8. **Kloppenborg's three-stage Q stratification is a recognized scholarly model** — *The Formation of Q* (2007) is a landmark contribution to Q scholarship cited extensively across peer-reviewed NT literature.

9. **Epiphanius (Panarion 29) references a Nazarene sect predating Christ** — The Panarion passage 29.1.3–7 is genuine and accurately represented; the description of a pre-Christian sect using the name is real, though the historical interpretation of this passage remains debated. *Source:* Williams trans., Brill (2013), 117–118.
