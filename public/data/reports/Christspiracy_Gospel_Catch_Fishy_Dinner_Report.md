## SECTION B — SCHOPENHAUER STRATAGEM ANALYSIS

### Stratagem 11 — Induction with Concealed Counterexamples
**Timestamp:** 5:37–6:17
**Exact quote:** **"In Matthew 16 and Mark 8, Jesus recalls multiplying the loaves alone. He doesn't mention the fish at all. That's huge… Fish doesn't even splash onto the manuscript record until 200 CE."**
Explanation: The speaker selectively cites recollection passages (Matthew 16:9–10, Mark 8:19–20) where Jesus mentions loaves and baskets — which is accurate — but conceals that the feeding narratives in all four gospels *in their primary accounts* (Matt 14:19, Mark 6:41, Luke 9:16, John 6:11) explicitly include fish. The "200 CE" papyrus claim further conceals that opsarion appears in John 6:9, 11 (the feeding of the 5,000) — a text the speaker's own analysis depends on. The induction ("the earliest sources only mention bread") is built by hiding the counterexamples.

### Stratagem 5 — Using Opponent's Beliefs Against Him (Applied to Audience)
**Timestamp:** 6:56–7:14
**Exact quote:** **"The long ending of Mark… is almost universally recognized even by apologists like Wes Huff as a later tack-on."**
Explanation: The speaker establishes consensus on the long ending of Mark — which is genuine — and then leverages the audience's acceptance of that point to imply that Luke 24 and John 21 are "running the same post-edit scene." The comparison is structurally misleading: the long ending of Mark lacks manuscript support (absent from Sinaiticus and Vaticanus), while no manuscript of John lacks chapter 21, and the Luke 24:42 textual situation is fundamentally different from Mark 16:9–20.

### Stratagem 23 — Extending the Opponent's Proposition Beyond Its Natural Limits
**Timestamp:** 14:03–16:22
**Exact quote:** **"Prosphagion doesn't necessarily mean fish. It simply means food or anything eaten with bread… Opsarion, same issue. While often translated as fish today, it originally meant any side dish or relish served with bread."**
Explanation: The speaker takes a legitimate lexical observation (these words have broader etymological roots) and extends it far beyond what the evidence supports. In NT-era Greek, opsarion had become a standard word for fish, as BDAG confirms. The etymological range of the root word opson centuries earlier does not determine the contextual meaning in a 1st-century fishing narrative where nets, boats, and ichthus appear in the same passage.

### Stratagem 19 — Generalizing from a Particular
**Timestamp:** 23:55–25:03
**Exact quote:** **"If Mary raised James that way, are we supposed to believe that she raised Jesus differently?… If Jesus had eaten meat, Paul could have easily ended the debate."**
Explanation: The speaker generalizes from Hegesippus's description of James's asceticism (a particular claim about one individual) to a universal claim about Jesus's practices. This assumes siblings share identical dietary practices — an unsupported inference. The argument from Paul's silence (he never cites Jesus eating meat) is similarly an unwarranted generalization from absence of evidence.

### Stratagem 12 — Strategic Word Choice to Prejudge the Issue
**Timestamp:** Throughout, but especially 14:03–14:28
**Exact quote:** **"Prosphagion… simply means food… Opsarion… originally meant any side dish or relish."**
Explanation: By consistently translating opsarion as "relish" and prosphagion as "food," the speaker has pre-loaded the conclusion. Standard NT lexicons (BDAG, Thayer's, Strong's) render both words with "fish" as the primary or co-primary meaning in their NT contexts. The choice of "relish" throughout the video is a rhetorical strategy, not a neutral lexical rendering.

### Stratagem 30 — Appeal to Authority (Misattributed or Unverifiable)
**Timestamp:** 16:04–16:14
**Exact quote:** **"Finicius Arabius, a 2nd century CE Greek grammarian, confirmed that opson wasn't just fish."**
Explanation: No ancient author named "Finicius Arabius" can be identified in classical scholarship. This appears to be a garbled reference to Athenaeus of Naucratis (2nd–3rd century CE) or possibly Julius Pollux (2nd century CE grammarian). Citing an unverifiable authority by an incorrect name to support a core claim is a form of false appeal to authority, whether intentional or not.

Evidence: NA28 apparatus; P66 and P75 manuscript evidence.
