## Source Context Verification Notes

### Matthew 15:1–9
Ortlund uses this passage to argue that Jesus drew a meaningful distinction between the "word of God" and "human tradition," with the former regulating the latter. This is a fair reading of the passage in its immediate context. Catholic and Orthodox scholars would counter that the Pharisaic traditions Jesus critiques are not equivalent to Sacred Tradition as understood in those communions — Jesus is critiquing human innovations that contradict divine commandments, not authoritative apostolic tradition. Ortlund partially anticipates this by framing the passage as a question ("do you grant that it's at least a meaningful conceptual distinction?") rather than a proof. Fair usage, though the applicability to the sola scriptura debate is contested.

### Basil the Great, *On the Holy Spirit*
The specific claim Ortlund makes — that Basil, while appealing to tradition, subordinated it to scripture — has textual support in the passage cited. However, chapter 27 (§66) of the same work explicitly defends unwritten apostolic traditions as having "the same force" as written scripture. This is not a marginal aside but one of the most frequently cited patristic passages in Catholic and Orthodox defenses of tradition. Ortlund's mitigation ("the historical evidence is messy"; "none of them are sola scriptura in a pure and proper sense") is honest and significant, but the omission of chapter 27 from the very work being cited remains a notable gap.

### Augustine, *De Unitate Ecclesiae*
Ortlund's rendering uses "descends" where standard translations have "dissents." This appears to be a verbal slip. The theological point — that Augustine anchored ecclesial identity in fidelity to scripture — is supported by this and other Augustinian passages. Augustine's broader corpus, however, also contains strong statements on episcopal and papal authority (e.g., *Contra Epistolam Manichaei* 5.6: "I would not believe the Gospel except as moved by the authority of the Catholic Church"), which complicate any straightforward Protestant appropriation.

## ADDENDUM — Scholar Gateway Academic Sourcing

The following peer-reviewed sources were retrieved via Scholar Gateway semantic search to strengthen the evidence base for key claims in this analysis. Citations follow the source hierarchy: peer-reviewed journals ranked above web sources.

### 1. Basil's *On the Holy Spirit* and Unwritten Tradition (Claim [16], Stratagem 11)

**Andrews, J. A. (2014). "'That the World May Know': A Christological Ecclesiology of Prayer." *Modern Theology*, 30(4), 481–499. https://doi.org/10.1111/moth.12095**

This article directly quotes Basil ch. 27 (§66) with the original Greek: "Concerning the teachings of the church, whether publicly proclaimed or reserved to members of the household of faith, we have received some from written sources, while others have been given to us secretly, through apostolic tradition. Both sources have equal force for the godly [πρὸς τὴν εὐσέβειαν]. No one would deny either source—no one, at any rate, who is even slightly familiar with the ordinances of the church. If we attacked unwritten customs, claiming them to be of little importance, we would fatally mutilate the Gospel."

**Pomeroy, S. (2020). "Patristic and Early Christian Biblical Exegesis." *Modern Theology*, 36(4), 884–899. https://doi.org/10.1111/moth.12630**

Discusses Emmanuel Amand de Mendietta's classic study showing Basil conceived of a "third strand" of tradition alongside "unwritten" tradition and the "secret" apostolic rule of faith (*The "Unwritten" and "Secret" Apostolic Traditions in the Theological Thought of St. Basil of Caesarea*, Scottish Journal of Theology Occasional Papers 13, 1965).

**Assessment:** These sources confirm that the omission of Basil ch. 27 in Ortlund's presentation is substantive, not incidental. Basil's defense of unwritten tradition as having "equal force" (ἴσην δύναμιν) with written scripture is from the very work Ortlund cites. Ortlund's qualifying caveats remain significant mitigating factors.

### 2. Oberman's Tradition I / Tradition II Taxonomy (Claim [12])

**Evener, V. (2019). "Reflections on teaching the tradition." *Dialog*, 58(4), 245–248. https://doi.org/10.1111/dial.12521**

Directly confirms: "Oberman calls the latter concept Tradition I (= 'the single exegetical tradition of interpreted scripture'), the former Tradition II (= 'the two-source theory which allows for an extra-biblical oral tradition')." Notes that the Reformation debates involved "competing concepts of tradition," not simply scripture vs. tradition.

**Assessment:** Ortlund's use of the "coincident view" terminology is academically grounded in Oberman's widely cited taxonomy. His claim that the earliest church held scripture and tradition as overlapping rather than as two separate sources is supported by mainstream historical theology. Claim [12] confirmed as True.

### 3. Augustine on Scripture and Church Authority (Claims [17–18])

**Smith, K. A. (2021). "The Sin of Heresy: Opposition to Heresy in Augustine's *Confessions*." *The Heythrop Journal*, 64(1), 111–119. https://doi.org/10.1111/heyj.13980**

Notes that for Augustine, "apostolic succession is the mechanism for ensuring this consistency" of tradition, and that "we can be confident that the Catholic Church, through its bishops, teaches true religious doctrines." Augustine's ecclesiology was deeply tied to episcopal succession, not merely scriptural fidelity.

**Timmermann, J. (2020). "An authority among authorities: knowledge and use of Augustine in the wider Carolingian world." *Early Medieval Europe*, 28(4), 532–559. https://doi.org/10.1111/emed.12429**

Shows that Augustine himself "recognized the need for a source of orthodox authority outside scripture itself, particularly in order to definitively interpret contentious scriptural passages." He "referred to consensus among major Christian writers" as an authoritative extra-scriptural norm.

**Assessment:** While the specific Augustine passage Ortlund cites from *De Unitate Ecclesiae* is real, the scholarly literature shows Augustine's broader ecclesiology was far more complex than a simple scripture-over-succession model. Ortlund's use of the passage is not false, but the scholarly context complicates the inference Turretin drew from it and that Ortlund endorses. This reinforces the report's original note that Augustine "also contains strong statements on episcopal and papal authority."

### 4. Immaculate Conception: Earliest Attestation (Claim [27])

**Riches, A. (2009). "Deconstructing the Linearity of Grace." *International Journal of Systematic Theology*, 10(2), 179–194. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2400.2008.00352.x**

Confirms that "not only Thomas Aquinas, but also Bonaventure, Albert the Great and Bernhard of Clairvaux all opposed the doctrine of the Immaculate Conception (which, of course, was not an article of Roman Catholic faith until the Bull of Pius IX in 1854)." Scotus (d. 1308) was the key clarifying figure.

**Davidson, I. J. (2008). "Pondering the Sinlessness of Jesus Christ." *International Journal of Systematic Theology*, 10(4), 372–398. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2400.2007.00331.x**

States that "virginal conception scarcely functions in such a fashion in the reflection of the first three centuries" — i.e., as a mechanism for immunizing Mary from original sin. "Scholastic accounts of original sin trade heavily in juridical and economic categories that are mostly alien to patristic thought."

**Weinandy, T. (2012). "The Annunciation and Nativity." *International Journal of Systematic Theology*, 14(2), 217–232. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2400.2011.00623.x**

Confirms the Eve-Mary typology in Irenaeus (2nd c.) but as a recapitulation/obedience theme, not as an Immaculate Conception doctrine.

**Assessment:** Ortlund's claim that the Immaculate Conception is "not seen until several centuries into the church" is confirmed by peer-reviewed scholarship. The 2nd-century Eve-Mary typology (Justin, Irenaeus) is not equivalent to the later Immaculate Conception doctrine. Even major medieval Catholic theologians opposed it. The doctrine was only dogmatized in 1854. Claim [27] upgraded from Disputed to Mostly True with scholarly backing.

### 5. Ignatius of Antioch: Dating, Apostolic Contact, and Ecclesiology (Claims [28–30])

**Holmes, M. W. (2009). Review of Allen Brent, *Ignatius of Antioch: A Martyr Bishop and the Origin of Episcopacy*. *Religious Studies Review*, 35(3), 193. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1748-0922.2009.01365_2.x**

Brent "convincingly dismantl[es] various recent proposals that the Ignatian letters are late second-century forgeries" and "situates Ignatius squarely in the middle of the society and culture of Hellenistic city-states in Asia Minor during the early part of the second century." Describes Ignatius's "distinctive views of church order" and his eucharistic theology as "viewing the Eucharist as a mystery play."

**Vall, G. (2013). *Learning Christ: Ignatius of Antioch and the Mystery of Redemption*. Reviewed in Ashworth, R. (2015). *Reviews in Religion & Theology*, 22(2), 197–199. https://doi.org/10.1111/rirt.12503**

Confirms "the date most generally suggested for these writings is sometime during the first two decades of the second century, although their christological and ecclesiological perspectives, especially the status given to the threefold ministry, have persuaded some to date them several decades later."

**Heckel, U. (2024). "Where Now for Visible Unity?" *The Ecumenical Review*, 76(3), 245–260. https://doi.org/10.1111/erev.12867**

Dates Ignatius to "110–114 AD" and notes "a uniform hierarchy of ministries is demanded only by Ignatius" — confirming Ortlund's claim about episcopal church government in Ignatius while noting this was "developed by Ignatius only in post-apostolic times."

**Assessment:** Ortlund's claims about Ignatius are well-supported. The traditional dating (c. 107–114 AD) is the majority scholarly view. Episcopal government and high eucharistic theology are confirmed features of the letters. The claim that Ignatius "knew the apostles" remains a traditional assertion with some scholarly debate (particularly from those who date the letters later), hence it stays Disputed. Claims [28] and [30] confirmed True; Claim [29] remains Disputed.

### 6. Sola Scriptura as *Norma Normans* in Confessional Protestantism (Claims [3–4])

**Ziegler, P. G. (2022). "On the Present Possibility of Sola Scriptura." *International Journal of Systematic Theology*, 24(4), 565–583. https://doi.org/10.1111/ijst.12568**

Confirms the technical formulation: Scripture's status as "*norma normas non normata*" (the norming norm that is not normed). Distinguishes the hermeneutical programme of sola scriptura from both *nuda scriptura* and from positions that deny scriptural primacy.

**Penner, B. M. (2017). Review of Matthew Barrett, *God's Word Alone*. *Reviews in Religion & Theology*, 24(4), 654–657. https://doi.org/10.1111/rirt.13041**

Confirms that "the magisterial reformers retrieved the patristic understanding of the supremacy of scripture as the ultimate authority over the church, including her traditions however sound and helpful they may be."
