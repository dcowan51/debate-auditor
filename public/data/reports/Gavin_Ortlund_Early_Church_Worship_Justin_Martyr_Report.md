## SECTION B — Schopenhauer Stratagem Analysis

This is a remarkably clean presentation. Ortlund is primarily descriptive and historical, not argumentative in the adversarial sense. However, one instance warrants flagging:

### Stratagem 11 — Induction with Concealed Counterexamples (Mild)

**Timestamp:** 7:22–7:52
**Exact quote:** **"there's no evidence of icon veneration in a building like this. There's no candles, incense, prayer gestures directed toward images, devotional setup, no indication of anything like that."**

**Explanation:** Ortlund frames the Dura Europos wall paintings as purely didactic/commemorative and argues the absence of cultic accoutrements (candles, incense, prayer gestures) proves no icon veneration. This is a reasonable argument from silence, but it omits a key counterpoint: the building was abandoned in 256 AD during a siege (it was filled with sand as a defensive measure), so the absence of portable devotional objects (candles, incense) cannot be taken as strong evidence about everyday use. Archaeological preservation conditions complicate arguments from absence. Additionally, the scholarly debate about what constitutes "veneration" versus "decorative" or "didactic" use of images in this period is more nuanced than presented. Ortlund acknowledges this is a contested topic ("we constantly distinguish between cultic use and didactic use"), but his framing implies the matter is more settled than it is among art historians and liturgical scholars.

This does not amount to a factual falsehood — the factual claims (wall paintings exist, no surviving cultic apparatus found) are accurate. But the inferential leap is somewhat stronger than the evidence warrants, and counterexamples from the scholarly discussion are not surfaced. Hence: Stratagem 11, mild.

Ortlund is notably direct in his claims. He hedges appropriately with language like "probably," "very likely," "seems to," and "the evidence suggests" — but these are legitimate epistemic qualifiers in historical discourse, not responsibility-transferring weasel words.

## SECTION H — Mostly True & Fair Claims

1. **Justin Martyr's First Apology dates to the 150s AD** [1] — Standard scholarly dating places it c. 155 AD during the reign of Antoninus Pius. ([Wikipedia: First Apology of Justin Martyr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Apology_of_Justin_Martyr))
2. **The First Apology is addressed to a Roman emperor** [2] — Addressed to Antoninus Pius, his adopted sons Marcus Aurelius and Lucius Verus, and the Roman Senate. (Ibid.)
3. **Sunday worship gradually replaced Saturday Sabbath gathering** [5] — Well-attested in scholarship; the shift is traced from the late first century onward. (Bauckham, *The Lord's Day*, 1982)
4. **The primary reason for Sunday worship was the resurrection of Christ** [6] — Justin himself says this in First Apology 67: "Sunday is the day on which we all hold our common assembly, because it is the first day on which God... made the world; and Jesus Christ our Savior on the same day rose from the dead."
5. **Constantine formalized Sunday as a day of rest** [7] — Edict of March 7, 321 AD. ([History and Headlines](https://www.historyandheadlines.com/march-7-321-sunday-became-christian-day-rest/))
6. **Justin lived in Rome** [8] — Confirmed by the Martyrdom of Justin, where he gives his address near the Timiotinian Bath. ([New Advent: Martyrdom of Justin](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0133.htm))
7. **Second-century worship largely happened in homes** [12] — Universally agreed in scholarship. Purpose-built church buildings do not appear until the late third/early fourth century.
8. **In the Martyrdom of Justin, he gives his own address but refuses to give others** [14] — Confirmed: he names his lodging "above one Martinus, at the Timiotinian Bath" but says he is "unaware of any other meeting." ([Wikisource: Martyrdom of Justin](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Ante-Nicene_Fathers/Volume_I/The_Martyrdom_of_Justin_Martyr))
9. **The Dura Europos house church was converted sometime around the mid-3rd century** [20] — Converted between 240–250 AD; destroyed/buried in 256 AD siege. ([Wikipedia: Dura-Europos church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dura-Europos_church))
10. **Dura Europos has wall paintings including the Good Shepherd and the healing of the paralytic** [22] — Confirmed; also includes Christ walking on water, Adam and Eve, David and Goliath, and the three Marys at the tomb. (Ibid.)
11. **Purpose-built church buildings appear dramatically in the 4th century after Constantine** [21] — Standard archaeological and historical finding. (White, *The Social Origins of Christian Architecture*, 1996)
12. **"Memoirs of the apostles" in Justin likely refers to the Gospels** [24] — Widely agreed; Justin uses this phrase elsewhere with clear reference to Gospel narratives. (Stanton, *Jesus and Gospel*, 2004)
13. **Tertullian (~200 AD) unites "the law and the prophets" with "the writings of evangelists and apostles" in one volume** [27] — Confirmed from *Prescription Against Heretics* ch. 36. ([CCEL: Tertullian](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/tertullian/heretics/anf03.v.iii.xxxvi.html))
14. **Pliny's letter to Trajan (~110 AD) references Christians singing a hymn to Christ on a fixed day before dawn** [42] — Confirmed: Epistulae X.96. ([Georgetown: Pliny and Trajan](https://faculty.georgetown.edu/jod/texts/pliny.html))
15. **The holy kiss appears in four letters of Paul** [44] — Romans 16:16, 1 Corinthians 16:20, 2 Corinthians 13:12, 1 Thessalonians 5:26. ([GotQuestions](https://www.gotquestions.org/holy-kiss.html))
16. **Didache 9 restricts the Eucharist to the baptized, quoting Matthew 7:6** [39][40] — Confirmed verbatim. ([New Advent: Didache](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0714.htm))
17. **The term "eucharist" derives from the Greek word for thanksgiving** [41] — Greek εὐχαριστία (eucharistia) = thanksgiving. Standard lexical fact.
18. **Colossians 3:16 references psalms, hymns, and spiritual songs** [43] — Confirmed verbatim in NRSVUE and other translations.
19. **Wine was mixed with water in the ancient world** [35] — Standard practice in Greco-Roman dining; extensively documented. (Dalby, *Food in the Ancient World*, 2003)
20. **The Eucharist was often embedded in a larger communal meal in the early church** [45] — Supported by McGowan, *Ancient Christian Worship* (2014), and widely agreed in liturgical scholarship.
21. **The catechetical process could take up to three years** [57] — Apostolic Tradition (attributed to Hippolytus, early 3rd century) prescribes three years. ([Encyclopedia.com: Catechumenate](https://www.encyclopedia.com/religion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/catechumenate))
22. **After the 4th century, the catechumenate was compressed** [58] — Confirmed; mass conversions after Constantine led to abbreviated preparation. (Ibid.)
23. **1 Corinthians 11:27–30 warns about unworthy participation in the Lord's Supper** [63] — Confirmed verbatim.
24. **Justin (ch. 66) requires belief and baptism for eucharistic participation** [64] — First Apology ch. 66: "no one is allowed to partake but the man who believes that the things which we teach are true, and who has been washed with the washing that is for the remission of sins."
25. **Nicaea II (787) addresses icon veneration** [62] — The Seventh Ecumenical Council specifically defined the veneration of icons. Standard church history.
