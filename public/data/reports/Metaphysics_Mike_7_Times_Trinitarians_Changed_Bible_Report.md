## SECTION B — Schopenhauer Stratagem Analysis

### Stratagem 11 — Induction with Concealed Counterexamples

**Timestamp:** 2:55–3:57
**Exact quote:** **"its inclusion likely represents a doctrinal effort to portray Jesus as ontologically divine which was critical to the evolving trinitarian Christology"**

The speaker argues that "theos" (God) in John 1:18 is a Trinitarian corruption of an original "huios" (son). He marshals supporting evidence: Alexandrian exclusivity of "theos," broad attestation of "huios," and patristic quotations. However, he conceals the single most decisive counterexample: the NA28/UBS5 critical text — the standard scholarly edition used by virtually all modern textual critics — reads "theos" as the original, rated {B} confidence. The scholarly consensus applies the principle of lectio difficilior (harder reading preferred) to conclude that "theos" is more likely original and "huios" represents scribal smoothing. By omitting this, the speaker inverts the actual state of the field.

### Stratagem 12 — Misleading Terminology / Choosing Metaphors to Favor One's Position

**Timestamp:** 0:02–0:36 (and throughout)
**Exact quote:** **"a pattern of textual corruption by trinitarians to support the Trinity"**

The speaker consistently frames normal textual variants — the kind that exist across all manuscript traditions for all sorts of reasons — as "corruption," "changed the Bible," and "outright corruptions." These terms prejudge the conclusion. In textual criticism, a "variant" is a neutral term; "corruption" implies intentional falsification. By loading the vocabulary from the outset, the speaker structures the audience's interpretation before evidence is presented.

The speaker takes a handful of Christologically relevant textual variants and extends this to claim that "all" major discrepancies surround Trinitarianism. In reality, the New Testament contains thousands of textual variants, the vast majority of which have nothing to do with Trinitarian theology — including variants in narrative details, word order, spelling, harmonization between Synoptic parallels, and so on. 2. Bruce Metzger's assessment that the "theos" reading in 1 Tim 3:16 arose accidentally or deliberately is an accurate paraphrase. — Metzger, *Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament*, 4th ed.
3. Modern English translations overwhelmingly do not read "God" in 1 Timothy 3:16. — ESV, NIV, NRSV, NASB all read "He" or "who."
4. The term "only begotten God" does appear in early manuscripts of John 1:18, including P66, Sinaiticus, and Vaticanus. — NA28 apparatus.
5. Greek manuscripts reading "theos" in John 1:18 are of the Alexandrian text type. — Confirmed by text-critical analysis.
6. Western, Byzantine, and Caesarean traditions predominantly read "huios" (son) in John 1:18. — Standard text-critical observation.
7. Tertullian quotes John 1:18 as reading "son." — *Against Praxeas* 15.6.
8. The author of John's Gospel uses "only begotten son" elsewhere (John 3:16, 3:18). — Primary text.
9. The oldest Greek manuscripts of Jude 1:5 (Vaticanus, Alexandrinus) read "Jesus." — NA28 apparatus.
10. A well-attested variant of Jude 1:5 reads "the Lord." — Codex Sinaiticus and other witnesses.
11. Old Testament passages consistently attribute the Exodus to "the Lord" (Yahweh). — Exodus 12:51, 20:2; Judges 6:8-9; etc.
12. The standard reading of Titus 3:6 is "through Jesus Christ our Savior" (without "God"). — All major translations and critical texts.
13. The Johannine Comma (1 John 5:7 KJV) is universally recognized by textual scholars as a later addition. — Metzger, *Textual Commentary*; Bruce, *The Epistles of John*; unanimous modern critical editions.
14. Bart Ehrman argues that scribes sometimes interpreted texts rather than merely transmitting them, and their interpretations occasionally entered the manuscript tradition. — Ehrman, *The Orthodox Corruption of Scripture* (1993).
