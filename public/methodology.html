Methodology

Every analysis follows the same documented process. No exceptions, no hidden criteria. Here is exactly how we score every video, paper, and book on this site.

Step by Step

The Analysis Process

Every video or paper goes through the same eight-step audit before a score is published. Nothing is scored until the full process is complete.

1

Full Transcript Review

We work from the complete transcript of the video — every word, not just highlighted moments. No cherry-picking from either direction.

2

Claim Extraction

Every atomic factual claim is pulled from the transcript with its exact timestamp and direct quote. These include: scripture citations, historical dates, scholarly attributions, statistical figures, and any assertion that can be independently verified.

3

Source Verification

Each claim is checked against primary texts, peer-reviewed scholarship, and reference-grade sources. We document what we checked and why the claim passes or fails. We note when evidence is genuinely mixed.

4

Claim Rating

Each claim is assigned one of four verdicts: True, False/Misleading, Disputed, or Not Checkable. Only checkable claims count toward the score.

5

Stratagem Detection

We identify rhetorical techniques that mislead without technically lying — cherry-picking, false dilemmas, loaded framing, equivocation. These are mapped to Schopenhauer's 38 stratagems, a documented taxonomy of argumentative misdirection.

6

Steelman Construction

We write the strongest possible version of the speaker's argument — the version a fair and knowledgeable advocate would make. This protects against strawmanning and is published alongside every analysis.

7

Threshold Rule Check

Six automatic rules can trigger a score override — for example, if a core false claim destroys the video's central thesis, or if the speaker misquotes a primary source. These rules are documented and applied consistently.

8

Score Calculation

True claims ÷ total checked claims = session score. Cumulative scores are tracked across all sessions for each creator, building a long-term accuracy picture.

Rating Scale

Claim Types

Every factual claim extracted from a transcript is assigned one of these four verdicts. Theological opinions and interpretations are excluded — only claims that can be checked against sources count.

TRUE

The claim is accurate as stated. The evidence supports it and there is no material qualification needed. True claims count positively toward the score.

FALSE / MISLEADING

The claim is factually wrong, or accurate in letter but structured to create a false impression — for example, quoting a statistic out of context to imply the opposite conclusion. False claims count negatively toward the score.

DISPUTED

Credentialed scholars are genuinely divided on this question, with defensible positions on both sides. Disputed claims are documented and disclosed but are excluded from the score calculation — we don't penalize speakers for landing on one side of a real scholarly debate.

NOT CHECKABLE

Claims that are purely interpretive, prophetic, or theological — "God intended this passage to mean…" — cannot be verified against external evidence and are excluded from scoring entirely.

The Math

Scoring Formula

The Truth Score is calculated from checkable claims only. Disputed claims and non-checkable claims are set aside. The formula is straightforward and applied the same way to every video.

True Claims ÷ Total Checkable Claims × 100 = Session Score

Example: 28 true out of 38 total checkable = 73.7% → rounded to 74%

Session Score

The accuracy percentage for a single video or paper. Every analysis produces one session score. This is what appears on each analysis card.

Cumulative Score

The running total across all sessions for a creator: all true claims ÷ all checked claims. This is the number that appears on creator profiles and the leaderboard.

Academic Standards Modifier

Written papers (not videos) are held to academic formatting standards. When a paper is presented in an academic context but omits basic scholarly conventions, a modifier is applied to the session score before it is published.

FAIL −1.5% per missing standard (institutional affiliation, abstract, literature review, citations)
PARTIAL −0.5% per standard that is present but incomplete
Score Bands

Verdicts

Every session score and cumulative score maps to one of three verdicts. These thresholds are fixed and apply to every creator and every video without exception.

80%+
Reliable

Mostly accurate. Minor errors on background or peripheral details. The central thesis and supporting claims hold up to scrutiny. Audiences can engage with this content with reasonable confidence.

60–79%
Caution

A significant mix of accurate and inaccurate claims. Some core or supporting claims are false or misleading. Verify specific assertions before citing or repeating them.

<60%
Unreliable

Significant factual errors, often in thesis-level claims. The content's core argument is likely false or constructed on a demonstrably inaccurate foundation.

Important: Verdicts are not about theological position

A speaker can score Reliable while arguing for a minority theological view, and a speaker can score Unreliable while holding a mainstream position. The verdict reflects factual accuracy, not whether we agree with the conclusion.

Not All Claims Are Equal

Claim Weighting

The session score is based on simple true ÷ total arithmetic. But we also run a separate Error Impact calculation — a weighted analysis that assesses how much the errors damage the video's core argument. This appears in every full report but does not replace the primary score.

CORE · 3×

Thesis-level claims that the video's entire argument depends on. A false core claim is the most damaging kind of error — it can invalidate the video's central point even if most other claims are true.

SUPPORT · 2×

Evidence claims that back the thesis — data points, historical examples, quoted scholarship. False support claims weaken the case without necessarily destroying it.

BACKGROUND · 1×

Incidental context — dates, biographical details, publication years, supporting color. Errors here are noted but carry minimal weight against the overall argument.

Error Impact = Weighted False Points ÷ Total Weighted Points × 100

Error Impact ≥ 35% flags the content as misleading overall, even if the raw claim count looks better.

Rhetorical Techniques

Schopenhauer's 38 Stratagems

Factual accuracy is only part of the picture. A speaker can be technically accurate while constructing a deeply misleading argument through selective framing, false dilemmas, or equivocation. We track these using Arthur Schopenhauer's The Art of Being Right — a 19th-century taxonomy of argumentative misdirection that maps cleanly onto modern online rhetoric.

Stratagems are noted and explained in every full report. They are not factored into the numerical score but are disclosed so audiences can evaluate the argument's structure, not just its data.

S1
Extension

Expanding the opponent's argument beyond what they actually claimed, then refuting the exaggerated version.

S2
Equivocation

Using the same word in two different senses to make an argument appear to follow when it doesn't.

S3
Generalization

Taking a specific, limited finding and applying it universally without justification.

S11
Concealed Counterexamples

Presenting an induction as if it applies universally while suppressing known exceptions that would weaken the conclusion.

S12
Pre-loading Terms

Defining key terms in an advantageous way before the argument begins, so the conclusion is already embedded in the definitions.

S33
Genetic Fallacy

Dismissing a position by attacking its origin rather than its content — explaining why someone believes something instead of engaging what they believe.

S35
Authority Appeal

Citing credentials or expert consensus as a substitute for argument, rather than as supporting evidence alongside argument.

+31
More in full reports

All 38 stratagems are documented. Each full analysis report discloses which were identified and why.

Score Overrides

Reliability Threshold Rules

Six automatic rules can override or flag a score regardless of the raw true/false count. These rules exist because not all errors have equal consequences — a single core misquote can be more damaging than a dozen peripheral errors.

🔴

Core-Error Rule

If a claim that is central to the video's thesis is demonstrably false, the score is flagged regardless of how many peripheral claims are accurate. Truth in the margins doesn't redeem a false foundation.

🔴

Pattern Rule

If false or misleading claims appear in a consistent pattern across multiple categories, the content is flagged as systematically unreliable — not just inaccurate on isolated points.

🔴

Misquote Rule

If a primary source (scripture, ancient text, historical document) is misquoted or fabricated, the score is flagged. Misquoting the sources the argument depends on is treated as a serious failure of scholarly integrity.

🟡

Decontextualization Rule

If an accurate quote is structurally removed from its context to imply a meaning the original source does not support, this is flagged even if the quote itself is verbatim.

🟡

Confidence Rule

Claims in a speaker's stated area of expertise are held to a higher standard. A biblical scholar misidentifying a book of the Bible carries more weight than a generalist making the same error.

🟡

Resistance Rule

If a speaker has been previously corrected on a claim and repeats it in a later session without addressing the correction, this is flagged. Repeated errors are treated as more significant than first-time errors.

What We Check Against

Source Hierarchy

Every claim is verified against the highest available source tier. We do not use Wikipedia as a primary source, and we do not accept a creator's own previous work as verification of their claims.

1

Primary Texts

Scripture in original languages (Hebrew, Greek, Aramaic), ancient documents, original manuscripts, papyri, inscriptions, and first-edition published works being cited.

2

Peer-Reviewed Scholarship

Journal articles and academic press monographs with peer review — biblical studies, ancient history, archaeology, linguistics, theology. Standard academic reference grammars (GKC, BDB, BDAG, Wallace).

3

Reference Works

Major Bible dictionaries and encyclopedias (NIDB, ABD, IVP), recognized scholarly commentaries, and encyclopedic reference works with documented editorial standards.

4

Reputable Secondary Sources

Wikipedia used only when paired with a Tier 1–3 source, or for non-contested background facts. Reputable journalism for contemporary events. Never used alone for theological or historical claims.

Boundaries

What We Don't Score

We do not score theological opinions, interpretations, or positions that are inherently a matter of faith rather than verifiable fact. This is intentional — and important.

✕

Theological interpretations. Whether a speaker believes Jesus is God, whether they hold to Calvinist or Arminian soteriology, whether they accept the Trinity — these are not scored. We check factual claims about what Scripture says, not whether someone's doctrinal conclusion is correct.

✕

Prophetic or experiential claims. Claims like "God told me…" or "I believe the Spirit is moving…" cannot be externally verified and are excluded from scoring.

✕

Minority scholarly views held in good faith. If a speaker adopts a minority position that has genuine scholarly advocates — even if most experts disagree — we document the scholarly split and exclude it from the score as disputed. We don't penalize intellectual honesty about contested questions.

✕

Editorial opinions and rhetorical framing. A speaker calling a position "absurd" or "obvious" is expressing an opinion, not a factual claim. These are flagged as weasel-words or stratagems where relevant, but they don't count in the score.

The Berean Standard

The Bereans in Acts 17:11 didn't reject Paul — they examined what he said against the Scriptures to see if it was true. We apply the same standard. Accuracy and theological diversity are not opposites. You can hold any theological position and still score Reliable. The question is always: are the factual claims you're making actually true?
